# Topic 10.2: Monte Carlo Schedule Risk Analysis (SRA) & Merge Bias

## 1. Executive Summary & Plain-English Analogy

### 1.1 The Flaw of Averages & The Three Commuters
Imagine three colleagues—Arjun, Priya, and Vikram—who carpool together from Jaipur to the Surya 100MW Solar IPP site in Bhadla, Rajasthan. Each person takes a separate vehicle to the highway junction:
* Arjun's drive takes an **average of 40 minutes** (uniformly between 30 and 50 minutes).
* Priya's drive takes an **average of 40 minutes** (uniformly between 30 and 50 minutes).
* Vikram's drive takes an **average of 40 minutes** (uniformly between 30 and 50 minutes).

They agree: *The carpool convoy cannot depart the highway junction until all three individuals arrive.*

```text
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                             THE COMMUTER MERGE BIAS PARADOX                                      │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                                                  │
│   Arjun (30 to 50 min, avg = 40 min) ──────┐                                                     │
│                                            │                                                     │
│   Priya (30 to 50 min, avg = 40 min) ──────┼──> [CONVOY DEPARTURE: max(Arjun, Priya, Vikram)]    │
│                                            │                                                     │
│   Vikram (30 to 50 min, avg = 40 min) ─────┘                                                     │
│                                                                                                  │
│   DETERMINISTIC CPM LOGIC SAYS:                                                                  │
│   Departure Time = max(40, 40, 40) = 40 minutes!                                                 │
│                                                                                                  │
│   STATISTICAL REALITY (MONTE CARLO PROBABILITY):                                                 │
│   For the convoy to leave in <= 40 minutes, Arjun AND Priya AND Vikram must ALL finish <= 40 min.│
│   P(Arjun <= 40) = 0.5                                                                           │
│   P(Priya <= 40) = 0.5                                                                           │
│   P(Vikram <= 40) = 0.5                                                                          │
│   P(Convoy leaves in <= 40 min) = 0.5 * 0.5 * 0.5 = 0.125 (ONLY 12.5% CHANCE!)                  │
│   Expected Convoy Departure Time = 45.0 minutes (NOT 40 minutes)!                                │
│                                                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

This mathematical trap is known as **Merge Event Bias**. 
In standard Critical Path Method (CPM) scheduling, project managers plug single "deterministic" numbers (e.g., 40 days) into their Gantt charts. Whenever multiple parallel paths merge into a successor milestone (such as commissioning or commercial operations), the probability that **all** paths finish on or before their deterministic dates decays exponentially.

This explains why **over 85% of global infrastructure mega-projects suffer severe schedule overruns**: deterministic CPM schedules are mathematically biased toward optimism!

## 2. Mathematical Foundations of Merge Event Bias

### 2.1 Jensen's Inequality and the Max Operator
In probability theory, the expectation of the maximum of independent random variables is strictly greater than or equal to the maximum of their expectations.

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$ be independent continuous random variables representing the durations or completion dates of $k$ parallel paths leading into a merge node. Let $T_{\text{merge}} = \max(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k)$.

Because the function $g(u) = \max(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_k)$ is a **convex function**, by **Jensen's Inequality**:

$$\mathbb{E}[\max(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k)] \ge \max(\mathbb{E}[X_1], \mathbb{E}[X_2], \dots, \mathbb{E}[X_k])$$

When $k \ge 2$ and the variables have non-zero variance, the inequality is **strict**:

$$\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{merge}}] > \max(\mathbb{E}[X_1], \mathbb{E}[X_2], \dots, \mathbb{E}[X_k])$$

### 2.2 Formal Proof for Two Uniform Random Variables
Let $X \sim U(0, 1)$ and $Y \sim U(0, 1)$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables representing the completion times of two parallel activities merging into Milestone $M$.

1. The deterministic CPM expectation is:
$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.5, \quad \mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.5 \implies \max(\mathbb{E}[X], \mathbb{E}[Y]) = 0.5$$

2. Let $Z = \max(X, Y)$. The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of $Z$ is:
$$F_Z(z) = P(Z \le z) = P(\max(X, Y) \le z) = P(X \le z \text{ and } Y \le z)$$
Since $X$ and $Y$ are independent:
$$F_Z(z) = P(X \le z) \cdot P(Y \le z) = z \cdot z = z^2 \quad \text{for } z \in [0, 1]$$

3. The Probability Density Function (PDF) is:
$$f_Z(z) = \frac{d}{dz} F_Z(z) = 2z$$

4. The true expected completion date of the merge milestone is:
$$\mathbb{E}[Z] = \int_0^1 z \cdot f_Z(z) \, dz = \int_0^1 z(2z) \, dz = \int_0^1 2z^2 \, dz = \left[ \frac{2z^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$$

$$\text{Error} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z] - \text{Deterministic}}{\text{Deterministic}} = \frac{0.667 - 0.500}{0.500} = +33.3\%$$

A deterministic schedule understates the milestone completion date by **33.3%** on a simple 2-path merge! In a solar plant with 15 parallel feeder lines merging into a central substation, deterministic CPM understates schedule risk by hundreds of days.

## 3. Probability Distributions in Schedule Risk Analysis

To replace deterministic durations with stochastic models, empirical risk modeling relies on three-point estimates:
* **Optimistic Duration ($a$ or $O$):** Everything goes flawlessly (1 in 20 chance, 5th percentile).
* **Most Likely Duration ($m$ or $M$):** Standard historical duration under normal operating conditions.
* **Pessimistic Duration ($b$ or $P$):** Major foreseeable risks materialize (95th percentile).

```text
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTIONS: TRIANGULAR vs. BETA-PERT                            │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                                                  │
│   TRIANGULAR DISTRIBUTION:                              BETA-PERT DISTRIBUTION:                  │
│             Peak (m)                                              Peak (m)                       │
│               /\                                                    .---.                        │
│              /  \                                                 /       \                      │
│             /    \                                               /         \                     │
│            /      \                                            .'           `.                   │
│           /        \                                          /               \                  │
│          /          \                                       .'                 `.                │
│   ──────'────────────`───────                        ──────'─────────────────────`───────        │
│        a              b                                   a                       b              │
│   (Piecewise linear; heavy tails)                    (Smooth bell curve; higher weight on m)     │
│                                                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 3.1 The Triangular Distribution
The Triangular distribution $T(a, m, b)$ is defined by a piecewise linear probability density function:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 
\frac{2(x - a)}{(b - a)(m - a)} & \text{for } a \le x < m \\ 
\frac{2(b - x)}{(b - a)(b - m)} & \text{for } m \le x \le b 
\end{cases}$$

Mean and Variance:
$$\mu = \frac{a + m + b}{3}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{a^2 + m^2 + b^2 - ab - am - bm}{18}$$

#### Inverse Transform Sampling for Triangular Distribution:
Given a uniform random variable $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$:
$$X = \begin{cases} 
a + \sqrt{U(b - a)(m - a)} & \text{if } U < \frac{m - a}{b - a} \\ 
b - \sqrt{(1 - U)(b - a)(b - m)} & \text{if } U \ge \frac{m - a}{b - a} 
\end{cases}$$

### 3.2 The Beta-PERT Distribution
The Beta-PERT distribution is widely favored over Triangular because it produces smooth, bell-shaped curves and assigns four times as much weight to the most likely value $m$, avoiding the artificially heavy tails of Triangular distributions:

$$\mu = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6}, \quad \sigma = \frac{b - a}{6}$$

The shape parameters $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ of the standard Beta distribution over $[0, 1]$ are:
$$\alpha_1 = 1 + 4 \left( \frac{m - a}{b - a} \right), \quad \alpha_2 = 1 + 4 \left( \frac{b - m}{b - a} \right)$$
A sample $Y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is scaled to duration $X$:
$$X = a + Y(b - a)$$

## 4. Key SRA Metrics & Confidence Percentiles

When running $N$ Monte Carlo iterations (typically $N = 5,000$ to $10,000$ iterations), each iteration draws random durations for all activities, executes a full CPM forward pass, and records the project finish date.

### 4.1 P50 vs. P80 Confidence Targets
* **P-Value ($P_k$):** The project duration such that $k\%$ of the Monte Carlo iterations finish on or before that date.
* **Deterministic CPM:** Frequently falls around **P10 to P25**. Bidding a commercial contract on the deterministic CPM date guarantees an 75–90% chance of default and liquidated damages!
* **P50 (Median):** 50% probability of on-time delivery. Used for internal target setting.
* **P80 (Enterprise Commitment):** 80% probability of on-time delivery. Used by lenders, investment committees, and EPC contracts.
* **Schedule Contingency Buffer:**
$$\text{Contingency Buffer} = P_{80} - \text{Deterministic Date}$$

### 4.2 Criticality Index (CI)
The **Criticality Index** of Activity $i$ measures the percentage of Monte Carlo iterations in which Activity $i$ fell on the Critical Path ($TF_i \le 0$):

$$\text{CI}_i = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^N \mathbb{I}(\text{Activity } i \text{ is critical in iteration } k)}{N} \times 100\%$$

* **Crucial Insight:** An activity with 20 days of deterministic total float can exhibit a Criticality Index of **65%** if its parallel paths have high variance!

### 4.3 Cruciality & Schedule Sensitivity Index (SSI)
* **Cruciality:** The Pearson or Spearman rank correlation coefficient between an activity's sampled duration and the total project completion date:
$$\rho_{\text{Cruciality}} = \text{Corr}(D_i, T_{\text{project}})$$
* **Schedule Sensitivity Index (SSI):** Combines duration variance, project variance, and Criticality Index:
$$\text{SSI}_i = \frac{\sigma_{D_i}}{\sigma_{T_{\text{project}}}} \times \text{CI}_i$$
Activities with high SSI are the true drivers of project delivery risk.

## 6. High-Performance Multi-Goroutine Golang Simulation Engine

To simulate 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations across a 500-activity project within 200 milliseconds, our Go engine uses:
1. Lock-free CPU worker pools sized to `runtime.NumCPU()`.
2. Independent pseudo-random number generator instances (`rand.New(rand.NewSource(...))`) per goroutine to eliminate mutex contention on global random sources.
3. Pre-allocated flattened integer arrays representing the DAG topological order.

```go
package sra

import (
	"fmt"
	"math"
	"math/rand"
	"runtime"
	"sort"
	"sync"
	"time"
)

// DistType represents the stochastic distribution model.
type DistType string

const (
	DistTriangular DistType = "TRIANGULAR"
	DistBetaPERT   DistType = "BETA_PERT"
)

// RiskDistribution holds the three-point estimates for an activity.
type RiskDistribution struct {
	Type        DistType
	Optimistic  float64 // a
	MostLikely  float64 // m
	Pessimistic float64 // b
}

// Sample generates a random duration from the distribution.
func (d *RiskDistribution) Sample(r *rand.Rand) float64 {
	a, m, b := d.Optimistic, d.MostLikely, d.Pessimistic
	if a == b {
		return a
	}

	u := r.Float64()

	switch d.Type {
	case DistTriangular:
		cutoff := (m - a) / (b - a)
		if u < cutoff {
			return a + math.Sqrt(u*(b-a)*(m-a))
		}
		return b - math.Sqrt((1.0-u)*(b-a)*(b-m))

	case DistBetaPERT:
		// Beta-PERT approximation using acceptance-rejection or gamma transform
		// alpha1 = 1 + 4*(m-a)/(b-a), alpha2 = 1 + 4*(b-m)/(b-a)
		alpha1 := 1.0 + 4.0*(m-a)/(b-a)
		alpha2 := 1.0 + 4.0*(b-m)/(b-a)
		betaSample := sampleBeta(r, alpha1, alpha2)
		return a + betaSample*(b-a)

	default:
		return m
	}
}

// sampleBeta generates a Beta(alpha, beta) random variable using two Gamma samples.
func sampleBeta(r *rand.Rand, alpha, beta float64) float64 {
	g1 := sampleStandardGamma(r, alpha)
	g2 := sampleStandardGamma(r, beta)
	if g1+g2 == 0 {
		return 0.5
	}
	return g1 / (g1 + g2)
}

// sampleStandardGamma generates Gamma(alpha, 1) using Marsaglia and Tsang method (alpha >= 1).
func sampleStandardGamma(r *rand.Rand, alpha float64) float64 {
	if alpha < 1.0 {
		// Boost alpha via Weibull property
		return sampleStandardGamma(r, alpha+1.0) * math.Pow(r.Float64(), 1.0/alpha)
	}

	d := alpha - 1.0/3.0
	c := 1.0 / math.Sqrt(9.0*d)

	for {
		var z float64
		var v float64
		for {
			z = r.NormFloat64()
			v = 1.0 + c*z
			if v > 0 {
				break
			}
		}

		v = v * v * v
		u := r.Float64()

		if u < 1.0-0.0331*z*z*z*z {
			return d * v
		}
		if math.Log(u) < 0.5*z*z+d*(1.0-v+math.Log(v)) {
			return d * v
		}
	}
}

// SimulationActivity represents a node in the Monte Carlo DAG.
type SimulationActivity struct {
	Index        int
	Code         string
	Distribution RiskDistribution
	Predecessors []int // Indices of predecessors
}

// SimulationResult captures the aggregated output of the Monte Carlo run.
type SimulationResult struct {
	TotalIterations  int
	DurationSamples  []float64
	P10              float64
	P50              float64
	P80              float64
	P90              float64
	Mean             float64
	StdDev           float64
	CriticalityIndex map[int]float64 // Activity Index -> Criticality % (0 to 100)
}

// MonteCarloEngine executes parallel schedule risk simulations.
type MonteCarloEngine struct {
	activities []*SimulationActivity
	iterations int
}

// NewMonteCarloEngine creates a new SRA engine instance.
func NewMonteCarloEngine(activities []*SimulationActivity, iterations int) *MonteCarloEngine {
	return &MonteCarloEngine{
		activities: activities,
		iterations: iterations,
	}
}

// Run executes the simulation concurrently across CPU cores.
func (e *MonteCarloEngine) Run() (*SimulationResult, error) {
	numWorkers := runtime.NumCPU()
	itersPerWorker := e.iterations / numWorkers
	numActivities := len(e.activities)

	type workerOutput struct {
		projectDurations []float64
		criticalCounts   []int
	}

	resultsChan := make(chan workerOutput, numWorkers)
	var wg sync.WaitGroup

	for w := 0; w < numWorkers; w++ {
		wg.Add(1)
		go func(workerID int) {
			defer wg.Done()
			rng := rand.New(rand.NewSource(time.Now().UnixNano() + int64(workerID*7919)))

			out := workerOutput{
				projectDurations: make([]float64, itersPerWorker),
				criticalCounts:   make([]int, numActivities),
			}

			// Pre-allocate forward pass scratch buffers
			earlyFinish := make([]float64, numActivities)
			durations := make([]float64, numActivities)

			for iter := 0; iter < itersPerWorker; iter++ {
				// 1. Sample durations
				for i := 0; i < numActivities; i++ {
					durations[i] = e.activities[i].Distribution.Sample(rng)
				}

				// 2. Forward pass (Assumes topological index order)
				var maxProjectFinish float64
				for i := 0; i < numActivities; i++ {
					var maxPredEF float64
					for _, predIdx := range e.activities[i].Predecessors {
						if earlyFinish[predIdx] > maxPredEF {
							maxPredEF = earlyFinish[predIdx]
						}
					}
					earlyFinish[i] = maxPredEF + durations[i]
					if earlyFinish[i] > maxProjectFinish {
						maxProjectFinish = earlyFinish[i]
					}
				}

				out.projectDurations[iter] = maxProjectFinish

				// 3. Backward critical path identification (tolerance of 1e-4)
				// An activity is on the critical path if earlyFinish + successors matches maxProjectFinish
				// For simplicity in this demo forward tracer, trace backwards from max finish
				for i := numActivities - 1; i >= 0; i-- {
					if math.Abs(earlyFinish[i]-maxProjectFinish) < 1e-3 {
						out.criticalCounts[i]++
					}
				}
			}

			resultsChan <- out
		}(w)
	}

	wg.Wait()
	close(resultsChan)

	// Consolidate worker outputs
	allDurations := make([]float64, 0, e.iterations)
	combinedCriticalCounts := make([]int, numActivities)

	for res := range resultsChan {
		allDurations = append(allDurations, res.projectDurations...)
		for i := 0; i < numActivities; i++ {
			combinedCriticalCounts[i] += res.criticalCounts[i]
		}
	}

	// Sort for percentile calculation
	sort.Float64s(allDurations)

	totalIters := len(allDurations)
	p10 := allDurations[int(float64(totalIters)*0.10)]
	p50 := allDurations[int(float64(totalIters)*0.50)]
	p80 := allDurations[int(float64(totalIters)*0.80)]
	p90 := allDurations[int(float64(totalIters)*0.90)]

	var sum, sumSq float64
	for _, val := range allDurations {
		sum += val
		sumSq += val * val
	}
	mean := sum / float64(totalIters)
	variance := (sumSq / float64(totalIters)) - (mean * mean)
	stdDev := math.Sqrt(variance)

	critMap := make(map[int]float64)
	for i := 0; i < numActivities; i++ {
		critMap[i] = (float64(combinedCriticalCounts[i]) / float64(totalIters)) * 100.0
	}

	return &SimulationResult{
		TotalIterations:  totalIters,
		DurationSamples:  allDurations,
		P10:              p10,
		P50:              p50,
		P80:              p80,
		P90:              p90,
		Mean:             mean,
		StdDev:           stdDev,
		CriticalityIndex: critMap,
	}, nil
}
```

## 7. Industry Pitfalls & Anti-Patterns

### 7.1 Pitfall 1: Assuming Deterministic CPM Critical Path is the Real Risk
* **The Anti-Pattern:** A risk consultant only assesses risks on the deterministic critical path, ignoring activities with 10 days of float.
* **Why it Breaks Systems:** Merge bias guarantees that near-critical parallel paths have a high probability of overtaking the deterministic critical path. Ignoring them leaves the project completely blind to parallel merge risks.
* **The Rule:** Always model duration distributions for all activities across the entire network, not just the deterministic critical path.

### 7.2 Pitfall 2: Over-Reliance on Triangular vs. Beta-PERT
* **The Anti-Pattern:** Using Triangular distributions for all tasks because "it's simple."
* **Why it Breaks Systems:** Triangular distributions assign uniform slope from $a$ to $m$ and $m$ to $b$. This places uncharacteristically heavy weight on extreme outcomes, overestimating project standard deviation by 15–25% and demanding excessive contingency buffers that cause developers to lose competitive bids.
* **The Rule:** Default to Beta-PERT for expert-judgment estimation; use Triangular only when empirical data is strictly bounded and uniform.

### 7.3 Pitfall 3: Shared Seed Mutex Paralyzation in Go
* **The Anti-Pattern:** Using the global `math/rand.Float64()` across 16 concurrent goroutines.
* **Why it Breaks Systems:** The global `math/rand` methods acquire an internal sync.Mutex on every single call! Under 10,000 iterations across 500 tasks (5,000,000 samples), 16 CPU cores spend 92% of their cycles blocked on mutex lock contention, dropping simulation speed by an order of magnitude.
* **The Rule:** Always instantiate a dedicated `rand.New(rand.NewSource(...))` per worker goroutine.
